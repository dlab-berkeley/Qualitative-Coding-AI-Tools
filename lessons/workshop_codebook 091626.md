# Bracero Oral History Codebook — Working Draft

This is the codebook participants will use for hand-coding, and the same one Codex will be prompted with beforehand to build the pre-computed results sheet the room actually sees. Two tiers. Five codes each. 
## How to use this document

Every code below needs a verbatim evidence span when it's applied — an exact quote from the passage, not a paraphrase. That span is what catches fabrication later: if the model claims a code applies and can't point to real words that support it, something's wrong, and that's the lesson, not an embarrassment to smooth over.

More than one code can apply to a single passage. Most of the good ones carry two or three.

## Tier 1 — Factual, extractive codes

These ask: what happened, where, and under what arrangement? A person with no background in oral history methods should be able to apply these correctly within a few minutes of instruction.

### T1-CROP
The passage names a specific crop, harvest, or agricultural product. Cotton, onions, corn, cherries. Apply this even when the crop is mentioned only in passing — it's a cheap, reliable warm-up code for people still getting their footing.

*Example (bha_1590_005):* "one of the crew chiefs, like a dollar was like $8.00 in Mexico" — actually skip that one, wrong code. Better: bha_1561_002, narrator recalls "we used to come home from school... and then you go out and work in the fields... to go out there and pick onions."

### T1-SITE
The passage describes a physical place — housing, a camp, a barracks, a clinic, a farm layout. Look for concrete detail: dimensions, materials, what the floor was made of.

*Example (bha_1574_001):* "It was pretty good size barn... eighty feet wide by about a hundred and twenty feet long. They were bunked at what they would call the hayloft."

### T1-CONTRACT
The passage references the formal terms binding a bracero to the program — wages promised, insurance, a set rate, the paperwork of legal status. This is the code most likely to overlap with T1-WAGE; when in doubt, apply both.

*Example (bha_1573_001):* "That insurance compromise that all the Braceros had to have five hundred dollar non-occupational insurance, five thousand dollar occupational insurance, and thirteen hundred dollars life insurance policy."

### T1-PROC
The passage describes a procedure or system in operation — how pay was logged, how a clinic ran, how recruitment worked, how discipline was handled. This is the workhorse code; more passages will carry it than any other T1.

*Example (bha_1593_024):* "we had their name up there, and the Hispanics, we had their numbers." A logging procedure, and a troubling one — good passage to sit with.

### T1-WAGE
The passage states or implies a specific pay figure, rate, or pay mechanism. Numbers are the tell here — dollar amounts, hourly rates, deduction amounts.

*Example (bha_1561_001):* "the paycheck was probably $2.00." Pairs directly against bha_1574_002, where the narrator insists there were no deductions at all — hold both in mind when teaching this code, because the contradiction between them is worth more than either passage alone.

## Tier 2 — Interpretive, affective codes

These ask something harder: what is the emotional or evaluative weight underneath what's being described? Tier 2 is where hand-coders will disagree with each other, and where they'll disagree with the model. That's expected. Frame it that way before anyone starts coding, or people will treat disagreement as their own mistake.

### T2-AMBIV
The narrator holds two feelings at once and doesn't resolve them — pride and pain, humor covering something uncomfortable, warmth alongside exploitation.

*Example (bha_1588_003):* "I know the merchants made... a lot of money from them, but I do remember some of them not being very nice to them." Both things are true in the same breath.

### T2-DIGNITY
The narrator treats the bracero as a full person with skill, agency, or worth — not as a labor unit. This code applies whether the narrator is a bracero's family member or an employer's family member; the test is what the narrator grants the bracero, not who's speaking.

*Example (bha_1582_002):* growers explaining well-drilling technique so workers could "take them back to their villages" — described as workers "looking for new ways to improve themselves," not as passive recipients.

### T2-NEGCOMP
Short for negotiating compliance, or its absence — moments where power is being exercised and the narrator either notices it or, often more tellingly, doesn't. Institutional accountability-dodging belongs here too.

*Example (bha_1573_004):* a sheriff deputizes braceros to fight an oil-well fire; when injuries follow, the company's response is "we are not responsible because the sheriff deputized them." Told without any apparent discomfort — that flatness is itself the code.

### T2-RETRO
A retrospective judgment on the program as a whole — good thing, bad thing, mixed. Distinguish this from T2-AMBIV: RETRO is a verdict, even a soft one; AMBIV is two feelings sitting unresolved.

*Example, positive:* bha_1573_005, "it helped the people in the United States and it helped the Mexican people." *Example, negative:* bha_1561_005, "pretty much legalized slavery is what happened." Use this pair as the anchor demonstration for the whole tier — same historical event, opposite verdicts, both from people one generation removed from it.

### T2-HARDSHIP
Left deliberately loose. This is not an oversight — it's the exercise. Give participants the four codes above with tight definitions, then hand them this one with almost nothing, and ask them to write the definition themselves after coding five or six passages. Watch where they draw the line between hardship-as-fact (low wages, cold housing) and hardship-as-feeling (a father's stomachache watching someone else eat, a child's shame at smelling like onions). Both readings are defensible. The gap between them is the lesson.

## Cross-interview pairs worth building activities around

- **bha_1561_001 vs. bha_1574_002** — direct contradiction on wage deductions, same general era, different farms.
- **bha_1561_005 vs. bha_1573_005** — negative vs. positive verdict on the whole program.
- **bha_1028 vs. bha_1116** — from the earlier Spanish-language draw; same wage deduction, described independently.
- **bha_1028 vs. bha_1116**, second angle — same reception center at Empalme, opposite closing assessments ("negativos, eso es ser uno un esclavo" vs. a net-positive read).
- **bha_1593_024 vs. bha_1593_027** — same narrator, two passages: differential recordkeeping by race, then differential wage-negotiation power by race. Strong single-narrator anchor pair.
