# Bracero Oral History Codebook

This codebook is for the workshop exercises in **Qualitative Coding with AI Tools**.

You will use the same codes when you code passages by hand and when you evaluate AI-generated coding later in the session.

The goal is not to force every passage into a category. The goal is to make a defensible coding decision and tie that decision to the text.

---

## How to use the codebook

A few rules apply throughout the workshop:

1. **Every code needs evidence.**  
   If you apply a code, copy the exact words from the passage that support it.

2. **No quote, no code.**  
   A good explanation is not enough if the evidence is not actually in the passage.

3. **More than one code can apply.**  
   A passage may describe wages, a workplace procedure, and hardship at the same time.

4. **You do not have to use every code.**  
   Do not stretch a definition just to make a code fit.

5. **Disagreement is useful.**  
   If two readers code the same passage differently, that does not automatically mean one person is wrong. Ask what part of the definition produced the disagreement.

# Tier 1 — Factual and extractive codes

Tier 1 asks a relatively concrete question:

> **What is explicitly being described here?**

These codes still require judgment, but the evidence is usually easier to point to directly in the passage.

---

## `T1-CROP` — Crop or agricultural product

### Use this code when

The passage names a specific crop, harvest, or agricultural product.

Examples might include cotton, onions, corn, cherries, or another named product.

A brief mention is enough.

### Evidence should look like

A direct reference to the crop or product.

### Example

**bha_1561_002**

> “we used to come home from school... and then you go out and work in the fields... to go out there and pick onions.”

The word **“onions”** is enough to support `T1-CROP`.

---

## `T1-SITE` — Physical setting

### Use this code when

The passage describes a physical place where people lived, worked, received services, or moved through the program.

This might include:

- housing;
- a labor camp;
- barracks;
- a clinic;
- a farm;
- a reception center; or
- the physical layout or material condition of a site.

Look especially for concrete details: size, construction, rooms, flooring, beds, buildings, or other features of the place.

### Evidence should look like

Words that describe an actual physical setting, not simply the name of a town or state.

### Example

**bha_1574_001**

> “It was pretty good size barn... eighty feet wide by about a hundred and twenty feet long. They were bunked at what they would call the hayloft.”

The passage gives a concrete description of the place where workers were housed.

---

## `T1-CONTRACT` — Formal terms or obligations

### Use this code when

The passage refers to the formal terms that governed a worker's participation in the Bracero Program.

This can include:

- promised wages;
- insurance requirements;
- deductions established by the agreement;
- legal status;
- formal employment conditions; or
- paperwork setting out rights or obligations.

### Evidence should look like

Language about what workers were formally promised, required to have, entitled to receive, or bound to do.

### Example

**bha_1573_001**

> “all the Braceros had to have five hundred dollar non-occupational insurance, five thousand dollar occupational insurance, and thirteen hundred dollars life insurance policy.”

This describes a formal insurance requirement attached to the program.

### Common overlap

`T1-CONTRACT` often overlaps with `T1-WAGE`.

If a passage gives a pay rate **as part of the formal employment agreement**, both codes may apply.

---

## `T1-PROC` — Procedure or system in operation

### Use this code when

The passage explains **how something was done**.

This might include:

- how workers were recruited;
- how pay was recorded;
- how workers were identified;
- how a clinic operated;
- how transportation was arranged;
- how discipline worked; or
- how an institution handled a recurring task.

This is a broad code. The important question is whether the narrator is describing a process, routine, or system rather than a one-time event alone.

### Evidence should look like

Words showing steps, routines, categories, recordkeeping, or repeated institutional practice.

### Example

**bha_1593_024**

> “we had their name up there, and the Hispanics, we had their numbers.”

This describes a recordkeeping procedure that treated groups differently.

---

## `T1-WAGE` — Pay, rate, or deduction

### Use this code when

The passage states or clearly describes:

- a wage;
- an hourly or piece rate;
- a paycheck amount;
- a deduction;
- a payment mechanism; or
- another specific way workers were paid.

Numbers are often a clue, but a number is not required if the pay mechanism itself is clear.

### Evidence should look like

A direct reference to money earned, money withheld, or the way payment worked.

### Example

**bha_1561_001**

> “the paycheck was probably $2.00.”

This is a direct statement about the amount left in a paycheck.

# Tier 2 — Interpretive and affective codes

Tier 2 asks a harder question:

> **How is the experience being framed, judged, or felt?**

These codes require more interpretation than Tier 1. Reasonable readers may disagree.

That disagreement is part of the exercise.

Your job is not to guess the “correct” answer. Your job is to make the strongest case you can from the passage itself.

---

## `T2-AMBIV` — Ambivalence

### Use this code when

The narrator holds two different evaluations or feelings at the same time without fully resolving the tension between them.

Examples might include:

- gratitude alongside resentment;
- warmth alongside criticism;
- pride alongside pain;
- humor around something uncomfortable; or
- recognition of both benefit and harm.

### Do not use this code just because

A passage contains both a positive fact and a negative fact.

The narrator needs to hold both in some meaningful tension.

### Example

**bha_1588_003**

> “I know the merchants made... a lot of money from them, but I do remember some of them not being very nice to them.”

The narrator recognizes economic benefit and mistreatment in the same reflection.

---

## `T2-DIGNITY` — Dignity, agency, or capability

### Use this code when

The passage presents a bracero as a person with agency, skill, knowledge, aspiration, judgment, or worth — rather than only as a unit of labor.

The narrator does not have to be a bracero for this code to apply.

The question is:

> **What kind of person does the passage allow the worker to be?**

### Evidence should look like

Language showing workers:

- making choices;
- learning;
- teaching;
- improving their circumstances;
- exercising skill;
- planning for the future; or
- being treated as people whose knowledge and aspirations matter.

### Example

**bha_1582_002**

The narrator describes workers learning well-drilling techniques so they could

> “take them back to their villages”

and describes them as

> “looking for new ways to improve themselves.”

The workers are represented as active learners with plans beyond the immediate job.

---

## `T2-NEGCOMP` — Negotiating compliance and power

### Use this code when

The passage shows how power is exercised, accepted, resisted, negotiated, or avoided.

This may involve:

- pressure to comply;
- unequal bargaining power;
- institutional authority;
- workers resisting or negotiating a demand;
- someone shifting responsibility to another institution; or
- a narrator describing an exercise of power without questioning it.

This code is deliberately interested in **who gets to decide, who has room to refuse, and who is held responsible**.

### Evidence should look like

Language showing a demand, order, negotiation, refusal, unequal choice, or avoidance of responsibility.

### Example

**bha_1573_004**

After braceros were deputized to fight an oil-well fire and workers were injured, the company response is described as:

> “we are not responsible because the sheriff deputized them.”

The passage raises questions about authority, responsibility, and who bears the consequences of an institutional decision.

---

## `T2-RETRO` — Retrospective judgment

### Use this code when

The narrator steps back and gives an overall judgment about the Bracero Program or a major part of the experience.

The judgment may be:

- positive;
- negative; or
- mixed.

The key is that the narrator is offering something like a **verdict**, not simply describing an event.

### How this differs from `T2-AMBIV`

`T2-AMBIV` is about unresolved tension between different feelings or evaluations.

`T2-RETRO` is an overall judgment, even if that judgment is qualified.

A passage can receive both codes if it genuinely does both.

### Examples

**bha_1573_005**

> “it helped the people in the United States and it helped the Mexican people.”

This is an overall positive assessment of the program.

**bha_1561_005**

> “pretty much legalized slavery is what happened.”

This is an overall negative assessment.

The two passages reach very different conclusions about the same historical program. The code captures the act of retrospective judgment, not whether we agree with the judgment.

---

## `T2-HARDSHIP` — Hardship

We are **not giving this code a complete definition yet**.

That is intentional.

As you work through the first set of passages, notice what you think should count as hardship.

You might find yourself asking:

- Does low pay automatically count?
- Does physical danger count even if the narrator describes it matter-of-factly?
- Does humiliation or shame count?
- Does hardship have to be explicitly named by the narrator?
- Should we code a difficult material condition, a feeling about that condition, or both?

For now, use your best judgment.

If you apply `T2-HARDSHIP`, you still need to provide an exact evidence span and be prepared to explain why you think the passage fits.

We will return to this definition later in the workshop.

# When codes overlap

Overlap is expected.

Here are some common combinations:

### `T1-CONTRACT` + `T1-WAGE`

Use both when a passage discusses a pay rate or deduction as part of a formal employment arrangement.

### `T1-PROC` + another Tier 1 code

A passage may describe **how** something worked and **what** it involved.

For example, a passage about how deductions were processed might receive both `T1-PROC` and `T1-WAGE`.

### Tier 1 + Tier 2

This will happen often.

A passage can describe a concrete fact and also carry an evaluative or emotional meaning.

For example:

- a dangerous work procedure may also suggest hardship;
- a wage deduction may also become part of a retrospective judgment;
- a workplace routine may also reveal unequal power.

The Tier 1 code identifies what is happening.

The Tier 2 code identifies how the experience is being framed or what kind of interpretive meaning it carries.

---

# A simple coding format

For each passage, record:

**Code:**  
`T1-WAGE`

**Evidence:**  
> “the paycheck was probably $2.00.”

**Why:**  
The narrator gives a specific paycheck amount.

If you cannot fill in the **Evidence** line with words that actually appear in the passage, reconsider the code.

---

## One last reminder

Qualitative coding is not a hunt for the one hidden correct answer.

A useful codebook does something more practical: it makes our decisions visible enough that another reader can understand, question, and reproduce them.

That is the standard we will use for both human coding and AI coding in this workshop.
