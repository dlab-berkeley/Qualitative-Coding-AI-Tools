# What did we actually learn?

The useful question is not whether AI is good or bad for qualitative research.

That question is too blunt.

The better question is whether we can make the model's work visible enough that a researcher trained in close reading, source criticism, and textual interpretation can decide what is worth keeping, what needs revision, and what should simply be rejected.

---

## Output is not interpretation

The model gave us codes, quotations, explanations, and ambiguity notes.

Those outputs can look like analysis because they have the surface features of analysis: labels, evidence, and prose.

But in humanities research, the presence of a quotation is not the same thing as an interpretation, and an explanation is not persuasive just because it is fluent.

The work begins when we ask whether the quotation is accurate, whether the code is warranted, and whether the explanation actually follows from the language of the passage.

---

## Close reading still matters

Close reading is not made obsolete by a tool that can summarize, label, or sort text quickly.

If anything, the exercise makes close reading more necessary, because the model can produce a claim so smoothly that the weak spot becomes easy to miss.

A single altered word, a missing sentence, or a quotation quietly stitched together from non-contiguous phrases can change the evidentiary basis of an interpretation.

That is not a technical issue first.

It is a reading issue.

---

## Textual analysis is not just extraction

Some of our codes were relatively concrete.

A crop name, a wage amount, a housing description, or a payroll procedure can often be located directly in the passage.

Other codes required a different kind of judgment.

Ambivalence, dignity, retrospective judgment, negotiated compliance, and hardship are not simply “things in the text” waiting to be pulled out.

They are interpretive claims about how the passage frames experience, agency, memory, power, and value.

That is exactly where humanities training matters.

---

## Three different problems

We saw at least three kinds of problems, and they should not be collapsed into one vague complaint that “the AI was wrong.”

Sometimes the quotation itself is not exact.

Sometimes the quotation is real, but the code attached to it is weak.

Sometimes the passage genuinely allows more than one defensible reading, because the language is ambivalent, the historical perspective is partial, or the code definition is not yet precise enough.

Each case calls for a different response.

---

## The source remains the authority

The corpus is not a decoration.

It is the ground of the exercise.

The model's answer only matters insofar as it can be checked against the source text, and the source text remains the place where the research claim has to return.

For archival work, oral history, literary analysis, translation studies, religious studies, history, anthropology, and related fields, this point is not optional.

The material itself has authority that the model does not have.

---

## A real quotation can still produce a bad reading

One of the more useful lessons is that accuracy at the level of quotation does not automatically solve interpretation.

The quoted words may really be there.

The evidence span may be copied correctly.

Still, the code may be too broad, too narrow, too eager, or simply pointed in the wrong direction.

This is why qualitative coding cannot be reduced to evidence retrieval.

The researcher still has to decide what the evidence means.

---

## The codebook is part of the method

The codebook is not just a list of tags.

It is a research instrument.

If a code is vague, the model will use that vagueness.

So will human readers.

The instability we saw around `T2-HARDSHIP` is therefore not merely a model problem; it is a sign that the category itself needed firmer boundaries, clearer examples, and a better account of what kind of evidence should count.

---

## What AI was useful for

AI was useful today because it produced a first pass quickly and made patterns of agreement and disagreement easier to see.

It gave us an object to argue with.

It also helped expose where the codebook was underspecified, where evidence rules needed to be stricter, and where a passage invited more than one plausible reading.

That is a real use.

But it is a methodological use, not a replacement for interpretation.

---

## What AI did not replace

It did not decide what mattered in the archive.

It did not know what would count as adequate evidence for a humanities argument.

It did not understand the ethical weight of testimony, memory, labor history, or migration history.

It did not decide how broad or narrow our categories should be.

Those decisions still belong to the researcher.

---

## Other humanities use cases

This workflow is not limited to oral history.

A similar process could be used to test a codebook for literary themes, rhetorical patterns, descriptions of ritual practice, representations of gender, moments of narrative self-correction, images of the body, scenes of reading, accounts of conversion, or references to law, kinship, labor, or debt.

It could also help with preliminary sorting in a large archive, comparison across interviews, identification of passages for closer reading, or stress-testing interpretive categories before they are applied to a larger corpus.

The key is that the AI pass should create a trail back to the text, not a shortcut around it.

---

## A workflow worth taking seriously

Start with a small corpus.

Read it yourself.

Draft the codebook.

Hand-code enough material to discover where the definitions break down.

Then let the model apply the same codebook, require evidence, and audit the output against the source.

Only after that should you decide whether the workflow deserves to scale.

The sequence matters because the mistakes are most useful while the corpus is still small enough to read closely.

---

## Before using this on your own research

Ask whether the material can be placed in the tool at all.

Ask whether the workflow could be described honestly in a methods section.

Ask whether you can check the evidence without trusting the model.

Ask what kind of error would be acceptable, what kind would be fatal, and what parts of the interpretive work you are not willing to delegate.

Those questions are not obstacles to using AI.

They are what make the use of AI methodologically serious.

---

## The question to leave with

The question is not simply:

Should I use AI?

A better question is:

Which parts of my research process could benefit from a fast, imperfect, evidence-generating first pass, and which parts require the slower judgment of a reader who understands the source, the archive, the field, and the stakes of the interpretation?
